Codex gpt-6-astra A8 opened 36 primary sources; the most load-bearing rule is to format alternatives as bullets, because numbered choices can be mistaken for required steps.

Evidence labels: **M** = measured; **C** = reasoned craft or practice guidance; **A** = asserted prescription or documented contract, with normative standards identified separately. “Checkable” means deterministically from Markdown source; it does not mean an automatic accessibility verdict.

**1. Line length — Let ordinary prose reflow; do not use source wrapping to enforce a screen measure.**

Bringhurst’s §2.1.2 gives **45–75 characters** for single-column serif text, **66 including spaces** as ideal, and **40–50** for multiple columns. I read [Rutter’s reproduction](https://github.com/clagnut/webtypography/blob/master/items/2.1.2.html), **a secondary quotation, not the original book**. [Butterick, “Line length”](https://practicaltypography.com/line-length.html), recommends **45–90 including spaces**, or **2–3 alphabets**: longer return sweeps make tracking the next line harder. **C:** neither passage supplies experimental derivation of its numbers.
Without template/CSS control, Markdown authors control paragraphs, nesting, hard breaks, and potentially wide tables/code/images—not ordinary rendered width. **Checkable:** source widths and forced breaks, not screen measure. [WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.8, AAA](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#visual-presentation), requires a mechanism permitting ≤80 characters, or 40 CJK; these need not be the default settings. **A, normative.** Plausible failure: losing a line containing an exception; not established by these numeric recommendations.

**2. Semantic line breaks — Start each sentence on a new source line; optionally break clauses without changing rendering.**

[SemBr, specification and “Why Use Semantic Line Breaks?”](https://sembr.org/), explains smaller diffs, localized edits, clearer source structure, and unchanged rendered paragraphs; it also recommends an **80-character source maximum**, with exceptions. [Write the Docs, “Semantic Linefeeds”](https://github.com/writethedocs/www/blob/main/docs/blog/newsletter-march-2023.rst), identifies one-sentence-per-line as “ventilated prose” and records both advocates and objections. [Uber Go Guide, “Line breaks”](https://github.com/uber-go/guide/blob/master/CONTRIBUTING.md#line-breaks), explicitly requires SemBr.
**C** for review benefits; **A** for project conventions, with no reader-outcome experiment in these sources. **Checkable:** hard-break syntax and before/after parsed-output equivalence; sentence/clause boundaries only heuristically. Avoid Markdown’s two trailing spaces or backslash when intending an invisible break. Wrong-answer connection is indirect: cleaner reviews may prevent an accidentally changed qualification; rendered readers receive no visual benefit.

**3. Headings — Encode the actual hierarchy, and make each heading identify the material it governs.**

[WAI, “Heading ranks”](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/page-structure/headings/), advises against descending from H2 directly to H4; returning from H4 to H2 is legitimate. [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) requires represented structure under **1.3.1, A**, descriptive headings under **2.4.6, AA**, and section headings under **2.4.10, AAA**. **A, normative:** these are not a universal three-level limit or a blanket failure for every skipped rank. [APG, “Structural Roles”](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/practices/structural-roles/), favors native HTML headings; [APG’s introduction](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/about/introduction/#apg-is-not-a-normative-standard) explicitly says APG itself is informative, unlike WCAG and ARIA.
[GitHub’s style guide, “Headers”](https://docs.github.com/en/contributing/style-guide-and-content-model/style-guide#headers), uses H2–H4 and permits questions; that is **A, house policy**. [W3C’s SC 2.4.6 explanation, “Intent”](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/headings-and-labels.html), says even one word may suffice. No measured universal heading-length ceiling or question-versus-noun-phrase winner was found. **Checkable:** heading nodes, ranks, duplicates and configured limits; descriptive accuracy requires judgment. Concrete failure: optional setup under “Prerequisites” becomes an apparent requirement.

**4. Tables, lists and prose — Match the structure to the reader’s operation: compare, enumerate, or understand a relationship.**

[NN/g, “Comparison Tables,” decision-making and scannability sections](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/comparison-tables/), argues that aligned attributes externalize comparison, avoiding remembered values and repeated navigation. Use tables for comparable attributes; lists for discrete items; numbered lists for sequence/count. [NN/g, “7 Tips,” tips 2 and 7](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/presenting-bulleted-lists/), reports users mistaking numbered alternatives for steps they must all complete (**M, qualitative observation; sample size omitted**). It recommends prose for short enumerations and discourages nested lists (**C**, not a measured cutoff).
Keep connected explanations in prose rather than fragmenting their qualifications. Flatten nesting beyond two levels as a **review heuristic**, not a scientifically established boundary. Narrow tables demand horizontal scrolling and can separate values from headers; **SC 1.4.10, AA** permits necessary two-dimensional data tables, but excludes individual cells from that exception. **Checkable:** nesting depth, table shape, headers and list type; appropriate structure and actual overflow are not source-only judgments. Concrete failures: “choose one” becomes “do all,” or a permission value is assigned to the wrong role.

**5. Warnings and callouts — Keep the warning visible immediately before the affected decision; use a box selectively.**

[GitHub, “Alerts”](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/writing-on-github/getting-started-with-writing-and-formatting-on-github/basic-writing-and-formatting-syntax#alerts), documents a standalone `> [!WARNING]` line followed by quoted body lines; available types are `NOTE`, `TIP`, `IMPORTANT`, `WARNING`, `CAUTION`. It recommends one or two alerts per article, no consecutive alerts, and no nesting. [Material, configuration, usage and collapsible blocks](https://squidfunk.github.io/mkdocs-material/reference/admonitions/), uses `!!! warning` with four-space-indented content; `???` collapses, `???+` initially expands. These require the documented extensions. **A:** syntax contracts and unmeasured quotas.
**C/synthesis:** preserve every decision-critical warning even when a quota would be exceeded; plain labeled prose can carry it. A box is not proven superior: [NN/g, banner blindness, “Visual Treatment”](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/), observed **26 participants** ignoring an inline promotion distinguished partly by its colored rectangular treatment (**M**, indirect evidence—not a warning experiment). I found no opened controlled comparison of identical boxed versus inline warnings. **Checkable:** syntax, nesting, counts, explicit warnings inside collapsed content; salience and correct placement require review. Failure: a reader executes the command while overlooking its destructive consequence.

**6. Collapsible details — Hide optional elaboration, not information needed to choose or perform the main action.**

[GitHub, “Creating a collapsed section”](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/writing-on-github/working-with-advanced-formatting/organizing-information-with-collapsed-sections), documents that `<details>` hides its body until expanded; `<summary>` remains visible, and `open` changes the initial state. [NN/g, “Accordions on Desktop,” usability issues](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-on-desktop/), explains added decisions, clicks, fragmented context and reduced discoverability. **C:** it provides no numerical cost estimate or controlled `<details>` experiment.
Find-in-page is browser-dependent, not universally defeated: [MDN’s compatibility dataset, `search_match_opens`](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/html/elements/details.json), records automatic opening in **Chrome 97+**, **Firefox 148+** (139–147 partial), and **Safari 26.2+ partial**, with incorrect scrolling noted for partial support. **A, documented compatibility; not tested here.** Source search still sees the body. **Checkable:** summary presence, open state and hidden descendants. Failure: an unseen platform exception turns a conditional instruction into an unconditional answer.

**7. Code blocks — Explain the operation first, tag the language, and distinguish commands from expected output.**

[GitHub’s style guide, “Code blocks” and “Outputs”](https://docs.github.com/en/contributing/style-guide-and-content-model/style-guide#code), places explanatory prose before code, requires language identifiers, discourages copyable `$` prompts, and targets **about 60 characters per code line** to avoid horizontal scrolling. [GitHub’s syntax reference, “Syntax highlighting”](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/writing-on-github/working-with-advanced-formatting/creating-and-highlighting-code-blocks#syntax-highlighting), documents the highlighting behavior. **C** for placement/copyability; **A** for the numeric house target and syntax.
Show short expected output when it lets readers recognize success; [Kubernetes’ style guide, “Separate commands from output”](https://github.com/kubernetes/website/blob/main/content/en/docs/contribute/style/style-guide.md#separate-commands-from-output), demonstrates separate blocks. No measured universal block-height limit was found. **Checkable:** fence closure, language tags, line counts/widths and prompt patterns; explanatory sufficiency is not. Failure: output is copied as a command, or an explanation after a long block is missed.

**8. Images and diagrams — Supply the information in text as well as its visual representation.**

[WCAG **1.1.1, A**](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#non-text-content), requires equivalent text alternatives, with exceptions including decoration; decorative images can use empty alt text. **1.4.5, AA** generally prefers actual text over images of text. [WAI, “Complex Images,” overview and long descriptions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/), explains short identification plus a description carrying essential values, relationships and trends. **A, normative requirement plus informative implementation guidance.**
**C:** use Mermaid for editable relationships when supported, raster for screenshots, and ASCII only where its source portability helps. ASCII is not inherently accessible: [W3C F72, “Description”](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/failures/F72), treats its spatial pattern as non-text content. [Mermaid, “Accessible Title and Description”](https://github.com/mermaid-js/mermaid/blob/develop/packages/mermaid/src/docs/config/accessibility.md), provides `accTitle` and `accDescr`; renderer support still matters. **Checkable:** image references, alt presence and Mermaid metadata—not equivalence or whether empty alt is justified. Failure: a diagram-only dependency disappears for a text reader; raster text also loses ordinary copying, searching and reflow.

**9. Links — Give the destination or action a meaningful label, while retaining URLs where the address itself matters.**

[WCAG **2.4.4, A**](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#link-purpose-in-context), permits purpose determined from link text **or programmatically determined context**. **2.4.9, AAA** requires a mechanism for identifying purpose from link text alone. **A, normative:** “click here” and bare URLs are therefore not automatic failures in every context; they fail when the required purpose cannot be determined.
**C:** descriptive labels support scanning and screen-reader link navigation; generic labels conceal destinations, while long URLs can create visual noise. **Checkable:** generic-label and bare-URL detection, local targets and heading fragments; these are review signals, not compliance verdicts. Failure: the reader cannot identify the link containing the exception and answers from incomplete information.

**10. Scanning — Provide distinct, descriptive headings and selective emphasis that guide readers to relevant sections.**

[NN/g’s original F-pattern study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/), opening methods paragraph, reports **232 users** across thousands of pages. Its [2017 revisit](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/), heatmap captions and explanations, includes **47 people** on the tiger page; it says F-scanning especially arises with weak formatting and limited motivation. **M:** an observed pattern, not an instruction to arrange content in an F.
[NN/g’s layer-cake article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/), definition, captions and heading guidance, shows a **9-participant heatmap** plus individual gaze plots; that is not a stated total study sample. **M** for observed heading scanning; **C** for prescriptions. Its “exponentially” improved usability claim has no quantified supporting effect there. **Checkable:** structural headings and emphasis spans, not gaze behavior. Failure: a visually undifferentiated exception is skipped during scanning.

**11. README length and first screen — Put purpose, applicability and the first safe route to use near the beginning.**

[GitHub, “About READMEs”](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-readmes), identifies what the project does, why it is useful, getting started and getting help; it documents heading-based navigation and truncation beyond **500 KiB**. **C** for content priorities; **A** for platform behavior. Putting these before large decoration is a synthesis, not a measured README-specific rule.
[NN/g, “Scrolling and Attention,” methods and results](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/), analyzed **120 participants**, **over 130,000 fixations**, on **1920×1080** screens: **57% of viewing time** above the fold, **74%** in the first two screenfuls, **81%** in the first three. **M:** these are attention shares—not percentages of readers who stopped; page lengths also affected the results. No universal README stopping point follows. **Checkable:** byte size and source order; repository chrome, viewport and rendering determine the actual fold. Failure: a reader starts from the opening example before encountering its prerequisite.

**12. Formatting can change answer accuracy — Treat scannability as a testable intervention, not a collection of sacred limits.**

[Morkes and Nielsen, “Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective,” Study 3 methods/results](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/), used **51 participants across five between-subject conditions**; **81** was the total across all three studies. The scannable condition bundled bullets, bold keywords, captions, shorter sections and more headings.
**M:** scannable users made fewer task errors than controls, **t(19)=2.16, one-tailed p<.05**; the reported **47% improvement was a composite usability score**, not a 47% comprehension increase. Individual formatting effects were not isolated. **Checkable:** presence of the features, not their causal benefit. This directly supports measuring wrong answers after a formatting repair.

**Bridge to `terse`.** Prioritize concrete hypotheses: numbered alternatives imply mandatory steps; a heading gives an exception the wrong scope; an offscreen table header assigns a value to the wrong option; a collapsed warning disappears from the decision. These map primarily to placement or findability failures. However, a model reading raw `.md` still sees collapsed text and HTML comments; it does not experience viewport width, visual prominence or human scanning. A raw-source score cannot validate those visual effects.

**deterministic checks**

- Parse the target Markdown dialect: inspect heading ranks, duplicate headings/anchors, list depth, table shape, fence closure and language identifiers.
- Resolve local links, fragments and image paths; flag generic link labels, bare URLs, missing alt attributes and absent Mermaid accessibility metadata for review.
- Detect alert types/nesting, `<details>` summaries and initial state, explicitly marked warnings inside collapsed blocks, and content placed in HTML comments.
- Report code widths, block lengths, nesting beyond two levels and GitHub’s 500-KiB README boundary; distinguish platform limits from unvalidated house thresholds.
- For semantic rewrapping, compare parsed output before and after. Do not turn sentence segmentation, punctuation, arbitrary length quotas or warning counts into correctness gates.
- Source checks cannot certify meaningful headings, alternative-text equivalence, correct warning placement or human visibility. Rendered overflow and browser-search checks can also be deterministic, but require a specified renderer/browser.
